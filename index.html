Cloth ERP case study

01At a glance

Problem

Five branches, their own factories and outsourced external factories were coordinated through Excel sheets and phone calls, so no one could see an order end to end.

Solution

One platform covering Certification, Sourcing, Production, Quality Check and Logistics, with role-based web views and a mobile app for floor tasks.

Impact

Live and in daily use across the business. [Add your strongest real outcome.]

02Context

A growing manufacturer had outgrown spreadsheets.

The company runs five branches, each with its own factory, and sends work to external factories when internal capacity is full. Orders move through many hands — from factory approval to dispatch — and all of it was tracked in Excel and through manual communication.

03The problem

Information broke at every handoff between teams.

Teams could not reliably see an order’s status, capacity, quality clearance or dispatch readiness, because each stage lived in separate sheets and messages.

How might wehelp teams coordinate orders, factory capacity, production, quality and dispatch across multiple locations through one clear workflow — without adding complexity to their day?

Business goals

  • Centralise operational information
  • Make capacity and allocation easier to manage
  • Standardise handoffs between teams
  • Support growth

User goals

  • Find order information fast
  • Know what needs attention next
  • Make allocation decisions with confidence
  • Record quality issues and rework clearly

Constraints

  • Multiple factories, plus external partners outside the company
  • Teams used to Excel and comfortable with it
  • Different roles working at desks and on the factory floor

04My role

I owned the product design end to end as the only designer.

I translated the business process into a product: mapping workflows, information architecture, user flows, UI for web and mobile, reusable patterns, and working with developers through build.

05Discovery

The spreadsheets were the best map of how the business really worked.

I worked from three sources: stakeholder discussions, analysis of the existing Excel sheets and documents, and synthesis of the workflow they described. There were no formal user interviews on this project. The sheets filled that gap: they showed what each team actually tracked, and where information was lost between them.

  • Insight 01

    The five modules are one connected lifecycle, not separate tools.

  • Insight 02

    Factory allocation depends on changing capacity, including external factories.

  • Insight 03

    Each stage needs a clear “done” state before the next team can start.

  • Insight 04

    Managers need a cross-factory overview; floor staff need fast task screens.

06Structure

The order became the single source of truth that every module connects to.

With the lifecycle mapped, I drew it as one flow. Every step reads from and writes to the same order, and quality check is the one gate that can send work backwards.

07Key decisions

Three decisions shaped the product.

Each one traded some simplicity in the build for clarity in daily use.

Decision 1

Make the order the central record

Options
Separate screens for each module, or one order record linked to every module.
Why
Every team needed the same truth, and users could follow an order without switching tools.
Trade-off
A richer order page that needed careful sectioning to avoid overload.
Decision 2

Separate order status from batch status

Options
One status per order, or a status for each batch.
Why
Batches of one order can be at different stages — one in quality check, another in dispatch.
Trade-off
More complex state design, in exchange for accurate partial progress.
Decision 3

Make quality clearance a controlled handoff, and split web and mobile by context

Options
Let any batch move to dispatch, or require quality clearance first; one interface everywhere, or web for planning and mobile for the floor.
Why
Failed or pending batches must not reach dispatch, and inspectors work on the factory floor, not at a desk.
Trade-off
Extra validation steps, and two experiences to keep consistent.

11Reflection

What I’d do differently.

  1. Test with factory staff earlier

    No formal user testing was done. Short sessions with inspectors and supervisors would have checked the mobile flows before they shipped.

  2. Agree success metrics before design

    Without a baseline from the Excel days, it is hard to show the change in numbers.

  3. Map exceptions sooner

    Rework, partial batches and capacity overflow shaped the product more than the happy path did, and they surfaced late.

What I’d change

Start the next project by sitting with floor staff for a day, and agree the two or three numbers that will prove the product works.
